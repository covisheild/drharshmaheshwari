## The rule in one line

**Keep your waist to less than half your height.**

The rule works whatever your height, age or sex, and all it needs is a measuring tape.

## How do I measure my waist at home?

1. Stand straight with your feet together. Measure over bare skin or a thin layer of clothing.
2. Find the **lowest rib** and the **top of your hip bone** on one side. Your waist is the point midway between them. For many people this sits a little above the navel.
3. Wrap the tape around your body at that level, keeping it flat and level all the way round.
4. **Breathe out normally**, then read the tape. Don't pull in your stomach.
5. Measure twice. If the two readings differ by more than 1 cm, measure a third time and take the average.

## What does my result mean?

Divide your waist by your height, both in centimetres. Or use the [calculator](/tools/bmi-calculator/), which does it for you.

| Waist-to-height ratio | What it means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.5 | No increased risk |
| 0.5 – 0.59 | Increased risk |
| 0.6 and above | High risk |

For example, if you are 165 cm tall with a 88 cm waist, 88 ÷ 165 = **0.53**, which is in the increased-risk band. Getting below 0.5 would need a waist under 82.5 cm.

## Why the waist matters more than the weighing scale

Fat stored deep inside the abdomen, around the liver and the gut, is chemically active. It pushes up blood sugar, blood pressure and liver fat. Indians tend to store more fat there, even when they look slim, which is one reason diabetes comes early in India.

A pooled analysis of studies covering over 300,000 adults found that waist-to-height ratio predicted diabetes, high blood pressure and heart risk better than BMI did.

## What about the Indian waist cut-offs I have heard of?

Indian guidelines also use a simple waist cut-off: **90 cm or more for men and 80 cm or more for women** signals abdominal obesity. The ratio adds a correction for height, which is fairer to very tall and very short people. The calculator on this site shows you both.
